# Academic English Writing: A Practical Style Guide

This guide adapts the usage rules and composition principles in William Strunk Jr. and E. B. White’s *The Elements of Style* (4th ed.) for academic and scientific writing. It follows the book’s broad progression—usage, composition, matters of form, commonly misused words, and style—then adds research-writing guidance for paper sections, citations, hedging, and disciplinary conventions.

The examples in this guide are newly written. The academic-writing additions are practical extensions and should not be read as rules from Strunk and White. Writing conventions vary by discipline, journal, and style manual; follow the requirements of the target venue whenever they differ from this guide.

## 1. Usage and Mechanics (adapted from Part I)

### Punctuation

#### The Comma

**Serial (Oxford) comma.** In a list of three or more items, many academic style guides (including APA and Chicago) use a comma before the final "and" or "or." Follow the target style guide.
- *"The study measured height, weight, and blood pressure."*
- Omitting it can create ambiguity: *"I thanked my parents, Marie Curie and Albert Einstein"* reads as if the parents are Curie and Einstein; *"I thanked my parents, Marie Curie, and Albert Einstein"* does not.

**Comma with coordinating conjunctions joining independent clauses.** When two independent clauses are joined by *and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet*, use a comma before the conjunction.
- *"The sample size was small, but the effect size was large."*
- If the second clause is not independent (no subject), omit the comma: *"The sample size was small but consistent across trials."*

**Comma after introductory elements.** Introductory clauses, phrases, or transitional words are typically followed by a comma.
- *"After adjusting for age, the correlation remained significant."*
- *"However, the second trial showed no such effect."*

**Comma with nonrestrictive (nonessential) clauses.** Use commas to set off information that adds detail but is not necessary to identify the noun. Omit commas for restrictive (essential) clauses.
- Nonrestrictive: *"The control group, which received a placebo, showed no change."*
- Restrictive: *"Participants who withdrew before week four were excluded."*

**Comma splices (error to avoid).** Do not join two independent clauses with only a comma.
- Incorrect: *"The results were significant, the effect size was small."*
- Correct: use a semicolon, a coordinating conjunction, or split into two sentences.

#### The Semicolon

- Joins two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction: *"The pilot study was promising; the full trial confirmed the effect."*
- Separates items in a list when the items themselves contain commas: *"We tested sites in Seoul, South Korea; Osaka, Japan; and Taipei, Taiwan."*
- Should not introduce a list or explanation (that is the colon's job).

#### The Colon

- Introduces a list, explanation, or elaboration, generally following a complete independent clause.
- Correct: *"The analysis revealed three key findings: increased latency, reduced accuracy, and higher variance."*
- Incorrect: *"The three findings were: increased latency, reduced accuracy, and higher variance."*
- Can introduce a clarifying or emphatic clause: *"The conclusion was unavoidable: the intervention had no measurable effect."*

#### The Em Dash and En Dash

- **Em dash (—)** sets off an abrupt break, emphatic aside, or parenthetical that deserves more emphasis than commas or parentheses. Used sparingly in formal academic prose.
  - *"The result — unexpected given prior literature — prompted a follow-up study."*
- **En dash (–)** is used mainly for ranges (numbers, dates, pages) and certain compound modifiers.
  - *"pp. 45–67"*, *"the 2015–2020 period"*, *"a Seoul–Tokyo comparison"*

#### Hyphens in Compound Modifiers

When two or more words function together as a single adjective before a noun, hyphenate them.
- *"a well-designed study"* (but *"the study was well designed"* — no hyphen when the phrase follows the noun)
- *"a two-way ANOVA"*, *"a high-frequency signal"*, *"a peer-reviewed article"*

Do not hyphenate adverb + adjective combinations where the adverb ends in *-ly*.
- Incorrect: *"a highly-significant result"*
- Correct: *"a highly significant result"*

#### Parentheses and Brackets

- Parentheses enclose supplementary information, in-text citations, or abbreviation definitions: *"Reaction time (RT) was measured in milliseconds."*
- Square brackets are used for editorial insertions within a quotation: *"The author stated that '[the] result contradicts earlier assumptions.'"*
- Avoid nesting more than one level of parenthetical information; if a sentence needs parentheses inside parentheses, restructure it.

#### Quotation Marks

- In American academic style, commas and periods go inside closing quotation marks; colons and semicolons go outside.
- Direct quotations of source material should be used sparingly in most scientific writing; paraphrase is generally preferred except when exact wording is analytically important (e.g., discourse analysis).

#### Apostrophes

- Possessive singular: add *'s* (*"the participant's response"*).
- Possessive plural ending in *-s*: add only an apostrophe (*"the participants' responses"*).
- Its vs. it's: *"its"* is possessive; *"it's"* is a contraction of "it is" and is generally avoided in formal academic prose.

### Grammar and Usage

#### Subject–Verb Agreement

- The verb agrees with the true grammatical subject, not with a noun that happens to sit closer to it.
  - Incorrect: *"The list of variables were long."*
  - Correct: *"The list of variables was long."*

- Collective nouns (*data, team, committee*) can be tricky:
  - **"Data"** originated as the plural of *datum*. Usage varies across fields and journals: some treat *data* as plural (*"The data suggest a trend"*), while others use it as a mass noun (*"The data suggests a trend"*). Follow the target venue and be consistent.
  - *"The team is..."* (single unit) vs. *"The team members are..."* (individuals).

- Neither/nor and either/or: the verb agrees with the nearer subject.
  - *"Neither the reviewers nor the editor was satisfied."*

#### Pronoun–Antecedent Agreement and Clarity

- Every pronoun should have an unambiguous antecedent.
  - Ambiguous: *"When the sample was added to the reagent, it changed color."*
  - Clear: *"The reagent changed color when the sample was added."*

- Avoid vague "this" or "it" referring to an entire preceding idea rather than a specific noun.
  - Vague: *"The model failed to converge in three of five runs. This suggests a problem with initialization."*
  - Clear: *"The model failed to converge in three of five runs, a pattern that suggests a problem with initialization."*

#### Dangling and Misplaced Modifiers

A modifying phrase should clearly attach to the subject of the sentence it introduces.
- Dangling: *"After centrifuging the samples, the supernatant was collected."*
- Corrected: *"After centrifuging the samples, we collected the supernatant."*

#### Verb Tense Consistency

- **Present tense** for established facts and statements about the paper itself: *"Water boils at 100°C at sea level."* / *"Table 2 shows the regression results."*
- **Past tense** for what was done or found in this specific study: *"We recruited 120 participants."*
- **Present perfect** for prior research considered as an ongoing body of knowledge: *"Several studies have examined this relationship."*

A common error is switching tense within a paragraph without reason.

#### Articles (a/an/the)

Article usage is one of the most difficult areas for writers whose first language does not mark this grammatical category (e.g., Korean, Japanese, Chinese).

- **"A/an"** introduces a noun for the first time or refers to any unspecified member of a category: *"We used a spectrophotometer."*
- **"The"** refers to a specific, previously identified, or uniquely identifiable noun: *"The spectrophotometer was calibrated daily."*
- **No article** with plural or uncountable nouns used generally: *"Data were collected over six months."*

#### Countable vs. Uncountable Nouns

Some nouns common in academic writing are uncountable:
- *research, evidence, information, equipment, knowledge* — do not pluralize or precede with "a/an."
- Correct: *"Further research is needed"* (not "researches").
- To express countable instances: *"a piece of evidence,"* *"a study,"* *"an item of equipment."*

## 2. Composition (adapted from Part II)

### Sentence-Level Principles

#### Prefer the Active Voice — With Deliberate Exceptions

- Weak (passive): *"It was found by the researchers that the treatment reduced inflammation."*
- Strong (active): *"The researchers found that the treatment reduced inflammation."*

**When passive voice is appropriate:**
- The agent is unknown, irrelevant, or obvious: *"Samples were collected at 24-hour intervals."*
- Method sections in the natural sciences, where the procedure is the subject of interest.
- To preserve a consistent topic across a paragraph (see the relevant section below).
- To avoid overusing first person where that convention is discouraged.

**Rule of thumb:** choose the voice that makes the agent, action, and topic clearest; avoid passive voice used only by habit.

#### State Claims in Positive Form

- Weak: *"The results were not statistically insignificant."*
- Strong: *"The results were statistically significant."*
- Wordy: *"We did not include participants who were not fluent in English."*
- More direct, if this exactly describes the eligibility rule: *"We included only participants fluent in English."*

Do not rewrite a negative claim into a positive one if the change alters the study’s inclusion criteria or scope.

#### Use Concrete, Specific Language

- Vague: *"Many studies have shown a relationship between the variables."*
- Specific: *"Twelve of the fourteen studies reviewed reported a positive correlation (r > 0.3) between X and Y."*

#### Omit Needless Words

| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| due to the fact that | because |
| in order to | to |
| a large number of | many |
| it is important to note that | *(often deletable)* |
| has the ability to | can |
| in the event that | if |
| at this point in time | now |
| conducted an analysis of | analyzed |
| in the majority of cases | usually |
| with regard to / with respect to | regarding / about |
| for the purpose of | to |
| in the process of | *(often deletable)* |
| it should be noted that | *(often deletable)* |
| a total of 40 participants | 40 participants |
| the reason why is that | because |
| despite the fact that | although |
| in a situation in which | when |

This does not mean cutting necessary hedges ("may suggest," "is consistent with") — those carry real epistemic content (see the relevant section below).

#### Keep Related Words Together

- Ambiguous: *"We analyzed the effect of temperature on enzyme activity using a spectrophotometer over six trials."*
- Clearer: *"Using a spectrophotometer, we measured enzyme activity across six trials at varying temperatures."*

Watch for stacked noun strings: *"the participant data collection procedure validation process"* → *"the process used to validate how participant data were collected."*

#### Use Parallel Construction

- Not parallel: *"The procedure involved recording temperature, to measure pH, and sample collection."*
- Parallel: *"The procedure involved recording temperature, measuring pH, and collecting samples."*

Applies to headings, hypotheses (H1/H2/H3 should share structure), and correlative conjunctions ("not only... but also"):
- Incorrect: *"The drug not only reduced symptoms but also was reducing relapse rates."*
- Correct: *"The drug not only reduced symptoms but also reduced relapse rates."*

#### Place the Emphatic Word at the End

- Buried: *"The difference between groups, although modest, was statistically significant."*
- Emphatic: *"Although modest, the difference between groups was statistically significant."*

#### Vary Sentence Length and Structure

A mix of long and short sentences prevents monotony; a short sentence after several long ones can land a key point with force.

#### Avoid Nominalizations Where a Verb Would Be Stronger

- Nominalized: *"The implementation of the intervention resulted in a reduction of symptoms."*
- Direct: *"Implementing the intervention reduced symptoms."*

| Nominalization | Verb form |
|---|---|
| conduct an investigation of | investigate |
| make a decision | decide |
| perform an analysis | analyze |
| give consideration to | consider |
| carry out an evaluation of | evaluate |

### Paragraph-Level Principles

#### One Idea per Paragraph

A paragraph usually works best when it develops one main claim or step in the argument. Treat this as a planning aid, not a rigid rule.

#### Topic Sentences Should Make a Claim

- Weak: *"This section discusses prior research on climate adaptation."*
- Strong: *"Prior research on climate adaptation has focused disproportionately on coastal regions, leaving inland vulnerability understudied."*

#### Maintain "Given–New" Flow

Well-constructed paragraphs open a sentence with information the reader already has ("given") and end with new information, which becomes the "given" for the next sentence.

> "The model was trained on 10,000 labeled images. **These images** were drawn from three publicly available datasets. **One of these datasets**, ImageNet-Sub, has previously been used to benchmark..."

#### Transitions Should Signal Real Logical Relationships

| Relationship | Transition words/phrases |
|---|---|
| Contrast | however, by contrast, conversely, whereas, on the other hand |
| Consequence | therefore, as a result, consequently, thus |
| Addition | furthermore, in addition, moreover |
| Concession | although, despite this, nonetheless, even so |
| Example | for instance, for example, specifically |
| Sequence | first, subsequently, finally |
| Similarity | similarly, likewise |

Avoid stacking synonymous transitions ("However, on the other hand...") and avoid defaulting to "also" everywhere addition is intended.

#### Paragraph Length

There is no universal paragraph-length rule. If a paragraph feels difficult to follow, check whether it contains several claims or needs more development; revise for the argument and venue rather than a word-count target.

---

#### Two further composition checks

**Choose a design and keep to it.** Before drafting, decide how the argument will unfold and make section order, paragraph order, and terminology support that design. Revise the design when the evidence calls for it.

**Avoid a succession of loosely connected sentences.** Make the relationship between adjacent claims clear through shared terms, logical transitions, or explicit explanation. A transition word cannot replace a real logical connection.

**Do not overstate.** Match the strength and scope of each claim to the evidence. This principle is especially important when summarizing results or prior studies.

## 3. Matters of Form and Citation (academic extension)

The original book has a short section on matters of form. In research writing, details such as headings, references, abbreviations, and numbers are usually governed by the target journal or style manual. The citation guidance below is an academic-writing extension, not a complete summary of that book section.

### Citation Prose and Manuscript Conventions

- **Integrate citations grammatically** rather than appending them awkwardly:
  - Awkward: *"The effect was significant. (Smith, 2020)"*
  - Integrated: *"The effect was significant (Smith, 2020)."* or *"Smith (2020) found that the effect was significant."*
- **Choose reporting verbs** that reflect what a source actually does (*argues, reports, suggests, proposes*). Do not vary verbs merely for variety, and avoid verbs that imply more certainty than the source supports.
- **Avoid citation strings with no synthesis**: *"(Smith, 2020; Lee, 2019; Kim, 2021)"* attached to a vague sentence gives no sense of what each source contributes; differentiate briefly when citing multiple sources together.
- **Match tense to citation convention**: APA typically favors past/present perfect for past research; some STEM fields favor present tense for findings treated as established fact.

## 4. Concision and Commonly Confused Words (adapted from Part IV)

### Concision and Word Choice

| Inflated | Plainer alternative |
|---|---|
| utilize | use |
| methodology | method (unless referring to the study of methods itself) |
| prior to | before |
| subsequent to | after |
| in the vicinity of | near |
| commence | begin/start |
| terminate | end |
| facilitate | help/enable |
| endeavor (verb) | try |
| ascertain | find out/determine |
| demonstrate | show (when no special emphasis is needed) |
| implement | carry out/do |
| optimal | best |
| sufficient | enough |
| numerous | many |
| approximately | about |
| in close proximity to | near |
| the majority of | most |
| a small number of | few |
| exhibit (a trend) | show |

This is not a prohibition on precise technical vocabulary — field-specific terms should be used when accurate. The caution is against **unnecessary** formality that adds no precision.

---

### Commonly Confused Words

| Pair | Distinction |
|---|---|
| affect / effect | *Affect* is usually a verb ("the drug affects mood"); *effect* is usually a noun ("the effect of the drug"). *Effect* can rarely be a verb meaning "to bring about." |
| which / that | *That* introduces restrictive clauses, no comma. *Which* introduces nonrestrictive clauses, set off by commas. |
| fewer / less | *Fewer* for countable nouns; *less* for uncountable nouns. |
| number / amount | *Number* for countable nouns; *amount* for uncountable nouns. |
| comprise / compose | The whole *comprises* the parts; the parts *compose* the whole. Avoid "is comprised of." |
| its / it's | *Its* = possessive; *it's* = "it is." |
| principal / principle | *Principal* = main/primary or head of an institution; *principle* = a fundamental rule. |
| complement / compliment | *Complement* = something that completes; *compliment* = praise. |
| discreet / discrete | *Discreet* = careful/tactful; *discrete* = separate/distinct. |
| farther / further | *Farther* for literal distance; *further* for figurative extent. |
| imply / infer | The speaker/writer *implies*; the listener/reader *infers*. |
| e.g. / i.e. | *e.g.* = "for example"; *i.e.* = "that is." |
| assure / ensure / insure | *Assure* = tell confidently; *ensure* = make certain; *insure* = arrange financial insurance. |
| criteria / criterion | *Criteria* is plural; *criterion* is singular. |
| data / datum | *Data* is technically plural, *datum* singular, though singular treatment is increasingly common outside formal scientific writing. |
| phenomena / phenomenon | *Phenomena* is plural; *phenomenon* is singular. |
| whether / if | *Whether* is generally preferred over "if" when introducing a choice between alternatives in formal writing. |

## 5. Approach to Style and Revision (adapted from Part V)

### Approach to Style (adapted from Part V)

Use these as prompts for revision rather than inflexible commands:

- **Write clearly and naturally.** Prefer a direct sentence that says what you mean.
- **Work from a suitable design.** Give the paper and each section a clear purpose and sequence.
- **Use strong nouns and verbs.** Replace vague wording and unnecessary nominalizations when a direct verb is clearer.
- **Revise and rewrite.** Clear prose usually comes from more than one draft.
- **Do not overwrite or explain more than the reader needs.** Keep details that support the claim, method, or interpretation.
- **Avoid inflated diction.** Choose a precise familiar word when it conveys the same meaning. Keep technical terms when they add precision.
- **Use qualifiers deliberately.** Do not stack hedges, but do not remove uncertainty that the evidence requires.
- **Make agency clear.** Readers should be able to tell who performed an action or made a claim.

### Revision and Self-Editing

1. **Pass 1 — Structure.** Read only each paragraph's topic sentence in sequence; check whether they form a coherent outline of the argument.
2. **Pass 2 — Verbs.** Flag passive constructions (deliberate or default?) and nominalizations.
3. **Pass 3 — Sentence length.** Mark especially long sentences (around 30–35 words can be a useful prompt, not a cutoff) and check whether they can be split without losing precision.
4. **Pass 4 — Word choice.** Scan for inflated diction, needless words, and commonly confused pairs.
5. **Pass 5 — Hedging calibration.** Check that claim strength matches evidence strength.
6. **Pass 6 — Transitions and flow.** Check that transitions signal the intended logical relationship and paragraphs follow given–new chains.
7. **Pass 7 — Mechanics.** Check comma usage, subject–verb agreement, article usage, tense consistency.
8. **Pass 8 — Read aloud.** Reading a draft aloud (or via text-to-speech) often surfaces awkward phrasing that silent reading misses.

Structural passes (1–2) are generally more efficient to do before fine-grained mechanical passes (6–7), since large-scale changes can make earlier line-editing wasted effort.

## 6. Academic Research-Writing Extensions

### Research-Paper Sections

#### Title
- Specific and descriptive, ideally containing key terms for discoverability.
- Avoid unnecessary phrases like "A Study of..." when a more direct title works.
- Avoid unexplained abbreviations.

#### Abstract
- States the problem, method, key result, and implication, usually in that order, typically 150–300 words.
- Avoid citations and undefined abbreviations.
- Every sentence should be interpretable on its own, since abstracts are often read and indexed separately.

#### Introduction
- Moves from general context → specific gap → specific contribution (the "funnel" structure).
- States the gap explicitly: *"However, it remains unclear whether..."*
- Ends with a clear purpose statement or hypothesis.
- A common pattern (sometimes called "Create a Research Space"): (1) establish the territory, (2) establish a niche/gap, (3) occupy the niche by stating the paper's contribution.

#### Literature Review
- Organize thematically or methodologically, not as a list of "Author (Year) found X. Author (Year) found Y."
- Synthesize: group studies by finding, method, or theoretical stance; discuss agreement, disagreement, and gaps.
- Choose reporting verbs (*argues, reports, suggests, proposes*) to represent what each source actually does; do not vary verbs merely for variety, and avoid verbs that imply more certainty than the source supports.

#### Methods
- Mostly past tense, often more passive voice, since the *procedure* is the subject.
- Detailed enough for replication: participants/materials, procedure, measures, analysis plan, usually in that order.
- Match the order of methods to the order results will later be presented.
- Briefly justify non-obvious methodological choices.

#### Results
- Report findings without interpreting them; save interpretation for the Discussion.
- Lead with the finding, not the statistical test name.
  - Weak: *"A t-test was conducted to compare the two groups."*
  - Strong: *"The treatment group showed significantly greater improvement than the control group, t(58) = 3.21, p = .002."*
- Use consistent decimal places/significant figures throughout.
- Figures/tables should be referenced in text but interpretable largely on their own.

#### Discussion
- Opens by restating the main finding in one clear sentence.
- Compares the finding explicitly to prior work: agreement, disagreement, or extension.
- States limitations specifically (sample characteristics, measurement constraints, design limitations), not as a generic disclaimer.
- Ends with a concrete implication rather than a vague closing statement.

#### Conclusion (if separate from Discussion)
- Should not simply repeat the abstract; typically reiterates the core contribution at a higher level and states its broader significance.

### Hedging and Claim Strength

Academic claims are rarely stated with absolute certainty; verb and modal choice signals exactly how strong a claim is.

**A rough progression in force (context-dependent):**
*may suggest* → *suggests* → *indicates* → *demonstrates*.

These verbs are not a universal scale: their force depends on the claim and evidence. Use *proves* sparingly, since empirical results rarely establish a claim without qualification.

"Proves" and "demonstrates" are strong claims reserved for well-supported conclusions; overusing them undermines credibility. Excessive hedging ("it might possibly perhaps suggest") stacks multiple hedges and reads as evasive — one hedge is usually enough.

**Common hedging devices:** modal verbs (*may, might, could, would*), epistemic verbs (*suggest, indicate, appear to, seem to*), adverbs (*possibly, potentially, generally, typically*), quantifying expressions (*some, many, most, in most cases*).

**Common boosting devices (for justified strong claims):** *clearly, demonstrably, it is well established that, the evidence strongly supports*.

Matching claim strength to evidence strength is one of the most field-general markers of mature academic style.

### Voice, Tense, and Person by Discipline

- **Natural sciences / engineering:** heavier passive use in Methods; first person ("we") increasingly accepted for actions authors specifically performed.
- **Social sciences:** mixed active/passive; first person common in qualitative work, more variable in quantitative work.
- **Humanities:** first person more broadly accepted; passive used more sparingly since agency is often foregrounded.

Most major style guides (e.g., APA 7th edition) now recommend "we" over impersonal passive when referring to authors' own actions, for clarity about who did what. Always check target-journal guidelines, since conventions differ.

### Common Issues in Academic English

#### Especially common for writers from article-less or classifier languages (e.g., Korean, Japanese, Chinese)
- Omission or overuse of "a/an/the" (see the relevant section below).
- Countable/uncountable confusion (see the relevant section below): "an equipments," "many informations," "researches show."
- Overuse of "very" where a stronger single word would be more precise.

#### Common across many L2 (and some L1) academic writers
- Run-on sentences chained with "and," "so," "but" rather than restructured.
- Overreliance on a small set of transitions ("also," "and also," "moreover") without matching them to actual logical relationships.
- Mixing formal and informal register (contractions like "don't," "it's" appearing in otherwise formal prose).
- Literal translation of idioms/structures from the writer's first language, producing grammatical but unidiomatic English — often best caught by a native-speaker proofreader or comparison against published papers in the same field.

#### Common even among native English academic writers
- Overuse of nominalizations (see the relevant section below).
- Overly long sentences burying the main clause under subordinate clauses.
- Excessive hedging or excessive certainty (see the relevant section below).
- Passive voice used by default rather than deliberately (see the relevant section below).
